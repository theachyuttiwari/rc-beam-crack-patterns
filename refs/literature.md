# State of the art: ML classification of crack patterns in RC beams (HOG + classical classifiers)

Compiled 2026-10-12. Every [key] is in references.bib and was checked against Crossref/doi.org or a publisher/repository page. Statements about a paper's content come from the metadata, abstract or page I could open; where I only saw a search snippet I say so. Leads I could not confirm are in unverified.md.

## a. Crack patterns as diagnostic evidence of RC beam behaviour

The idea that a beam's crack pattern tells you how it is failing is older than any image processing. Textbooks and committee reports describe three families. Flexural cracks start in the constant-moment region, are roughly vertical and perpendicular to the tension steel, and widen as the steel yields; the beam then fails by steel yielding and crushing of the compression zone, which is the ductile mode that design codes aim for [macgregor2012reinforced; parkpaulay1975]. Shear (diagonal-tension) cracks form at roughly 45 degrees in the shear span, away from the moment peak, once the principal tensile stress exceeds the concrete tensile strength; beams without stirrups can then fail suddenly [asce426shear; asce445recent]. Flexure-shear cracks begin as vertical flexural cracks and bend toward the load point as shear stress increases, and are the most common pattern in beams of intermediate shear span [macgregor2012reinforced; asce426shear]. Which pattern appears depends mainly on the shear span-to-depth ratio, the amount of longitudinal and transverse steel, and member size.

Kani's test series, summarised in "The Riddle of Shear Failure and its Solution", showed how shear capacity changes with shear span-to-depth ratio (the "shear valley") and that diagonal cracking does not always mean collapse [kani1964riddle]. The ASCE-ACI Committee 426 report collected the evidence on shear in members without and with web reinforcement [asce426shear], and its successor Committee 445 reviewed the models used in codes [asce445recent]. Empirical shear-strength prediction from a database of tests goes back to Zsutty [zsutty1968beam]. Mechanics-based alternatives include the modified compression-field theory, which treats cracked concrete as a continuum with tension-stiffening [vecchio1986mcft], and fracture-mechanics size effect, which explains why larger beams without stirrups are weaker in shear than small ones [bazant1984size]. Codes put these ideas into rules: ACI 318 for the US [aci318] and the fib Model Code 2010 for Europe [fibmc2010], both with commentary on cracking and shear mechanisms. Park and Paulay, and MacGregor and Wight, are the standard teaching references for the crack descriptions above [parkpaulay1975; macgregor2012reinforced].

Two points matter for an image classifier. First, crack patterns are evidence at the pattern level (orientation, spacing, extent along the span), not at the pixel level; a single crack segment does not tell flexure from shear, but the field of orientations does. Second, the three classes overlap: the transition from flexure to flexure-shear is continuous in shear span-to-depth, so label noise at class boundaries is physical, not annotator error. A paper should define its labelling rule (by observed failure mode, by cracking pattern at a stated load, or by design shear span) and say who labelled.

I did not locate in this search a source that gives the flexural / shear / shear-flexural taxonomy as a single canonical definition in the committee reports; the descriptions above follow the textbooks and Kani-era literature, and the paper should cite the exact textbook sections it relies on (to be checked against page numbers before submission).

## b. Vision-based concrete crack detection

Surveys frame the field. Koch et al. review computer-vision defect detection for concrete and asphalt infrastructure up to 2015 [koch2015review]. Spencer, Hoskere and Narazaki cover the broader inspection and monitoring pipeline including deep learning and UAVs [spencer2019advances]. Hsieh and Tsai review ML crack detection and compare model performance [hsieh2020machine]. Ali et al. review deep-CNN structural crack detection [ali2022structural]. Konig et al. review crack segmentation, detection and quantification with deep learning (arXiv preprint) [konig2022whats]. Flah et al. classify and quantify cracks with deep learning image techniques [flah2020cracks].

Classical image processing came first. Abdel-Qader et al. compared edge detectors (Sobel, Canny, fast Haar transform, fast Fourier transform) for bridge-crack identification and found the fast Haar transform most reliable in their test set [abdelqader2003edge]. Percolation-based processing grows a crack region from seeds and was designed for large concrete surface images [yamaguchi2010percolation]. Such methods need tuning to lighting and texture and typically output crack/no-crack at pixel or patch level. Yeum and Dyke combined image processing with a sliding-window approach for bridge steel-component cracks [yeum2015vision].

Hand-crafted features plus classifiers form the middle era. HOG, LBP, and their combinations feed SVM, random forest or boosted trees (section d, e). Zoubir et al. classify concrete bridge crack images with HOG and uniform LBP, kernel PCA, and SVM, random forest and decision tree [zoubir2022concrete]. Sun et al. use HOG with an SVM tuned by Bayesian optimisation for concrete crack detection [sun2023hog]. Wang et al. build an SVM crack recogniser from explicit crack features [wang2024svm].

The CNN era begins with Zhang et al., who trained a CNN on road-pavement patches [zhang2016road], and Cha et al., who trained a CNN on concrete patches and used a sliding window to localise cracks, with a comparison against Canny and Sobel edge detectors [cha2017deep]. Dorafshan et al. compared deep CNNs with edge detectors on concrete and found the CNNs better on their data, though edge detectors performed acceptably when tuned [dorafshan2018comparison]. Segmentation networks followed: DeepCrack uses hierarchical convolutional features [zou2019deepcrack]; Yang et al. use a fully convolutional network for pixel-level crack detection and measurement [yang2018fcn]; U-Net, originally for biomedical segmentation, is the usual backbone [ronneberger2015unet]. YOLO-family detectors are now common, but I did not verify a foundational YOLO crack paper (unverified.md).

What this literature mostly does not do is say what the cracks mean for the member. Almost all of it asks "is there a crack here?" and, at most, measures width and length. The next section covers the smaller body that goes further.

## c. Crack-pattern based damage assessment and failure-mode prediction of RC members from images

Beams and slabs. The closest precedent for our work is Davoudi, Miller and Kutz. In "Structural load estimation using machine vision and surface crack patterns for shear-critical RC beams and slabs" they estimate the applied load level from surface crack patterns, using test data collected from published studies [davoudi2018structural]. Their second paper in Automation in Construction gives a data-driven inspection framework for RC beams and slabs, with quantitative damage and load estimation [davoudi2019datadriven]. The associated Mendeley record holds 867 crack images of 129 specimens from 17 earlier studies (43 specimens with shear reinforcement) and includes classification models [davoudi2018data]. These works target shear-critical members and a load or damage-level output; they do not set out a flexural / shear / shear-flexural classification as the main task, to my reading of the abstracts.

Kumar and Sai (2026) study crack characterisation, severity and propagation prediction in RC beams with transfer-learning classifiers and report ResNet18 as best [kumar2026beams]. I saw only the abstract-level snippet (paywalled), so I cannot say whether their "crack types" align with flexure/shear; verify before citing for that purpose.

Columns, joints and walls. Most crack-image failure-mode work targets seismic members. Azhari and Hamidia identify seismic failure modes of damaged RC columns from crack images with data-driven models trained on a databank of surface crack patterns from quasi-static column tests (per the search listing: 455 patterns from 111 columns) [azhari2024crack]. Afzali et al. estimate a strength-based seismic damage index for RC columns from crack images [afzali2023damage]. Hamidia et al. do ML seismic damage assessment of non-ductile beam-column joints from crack images [hamidia2022joints]. Xu et al. identify seismic damage in RC columns from images with a region-based CNN [xu2019columns]. These show the pattern-to-mechanism idea is accepted, and they share the same data-scarcity structure: a few hundred images from a few dozen specimens, often many images per specimen at increasing drift.

General structural damage recognition. Gao and Mosalam applied deep transfer learning to structural damage recognition tasks (e.g., damage state, component type) [gao2018deep], and released PEER Hub ImageNet as a multi-attribute benchmark [gao2020peer]. These are scene-level image labels, not beam crack-pattern mechanics.

Tabular failure-mode classification. Mangalathu and Jeon classify the failure mode of RC beam-column joints from tabulated design parameters using ML [mangalathu2018classification]; Mangalathu, Hwang and Jeon apply SHAP to failure-mode analysis of RC members [mangalathu2020fmea]. They show classical classifiers (including tree ensembles) are standard for failure mode in structural engineering, but their inputs are design variables, not photographs.

A beam-image dataset with a failure class exists: Del Savio et al. released 1,132 laboratory-classified images with one beam class (bending failure) and four column classes [delsavio2025dataset; delsavio2025ulima].

Names suggested in the brief that I could not tie to a verifiable image-based failure-mode paper (Hwang, Zhai & Burton, Kakavand, Ghaffarian, Shi, Yang/Fang-style RC work) are recorded in unverified.md and are not cited.

## d. Hand-crafted features and why HOG suits crack orientation

Dalal and Triggs introduced HOG for pedestrian detection: gradient orientations are binned in small cells, normalised over overlapping blocks, and concatenated [dalal2005histograms]. A crack is a thin, elongated edge structure; its gradient is perpendicular to the crack direction. HOG therefore encodes the distribution of crack directions within each cell, with some tolerance to small shifts and illumination change through block normalisation. That is the property the three beam classes need: vertical flexural cracks, inclined shear cracks, and mixed patterns have different orientation histograms and different spatial layouts. (This is a design argument; I did not find a paper that tests it specifically for beam failure modes.)

Alternatives are textural. Haralick's grey-level co-occurrence statistics describe texture through pair-wise grey-level relationships [haralick1973textural]; LBP encodes local intensity patterns and has rotation-invariant, multiresolution forms [ojala2002multiresolution]. Gabor filters and Hu moments are other common choices; I did not verify citations for them beyond the pavement Gabor paper listed in unverified.md. In crack work, HOG and LBP are frequently combined; Zoubir et al. found SVM on fused HOG+ULBP features gave the best of the models they compared [zoubir2022concrete], and Sun et al. studied how HOG settings affect SVM crack detection [sun2023hog]. Note LBP and GLCM capture texture rather than orientation, so on photographs of concrete with strong surface texture (formwork marks, aggregate) they may encode nuisance variation; this is a hypothesis for an ablation.

The drawback of HOG is that it is local and sensitive to scale and to non-crack edges (rulers, markers, test rig, specimen boundary). Cropping and background control must be reported.

## e. Classical classifiers in SHM image tasks, and comparisons

The standard set is the support vector machine [cortes1995support], random forests [breiman2001random], k-nearest neighbours, decision trees, and gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost) [chen2016xgboost], usually through scikit-learn [pedregosa2011scikit]. Evidence on relative merit is mixed and dataset-dependent. Hsieh and Tsai compare ML models for crack detection [hsieh2020machine]. Zoubir et al. compared SVM, random forest and decision tree on HOG/ULBP features with SVM best [zoubir2022concrete]. In structural failure-mode classification with tabular inputs, tree ensembles and SVMs are routinely benchmarked [mangalathu2018classification]. CNNs generally outperform hand-crafted pipelines when data are plentiful [dorafshan2018comparison; cha2017deep], but with a few hundred beam images and few specimens a classical pipeline is a reasonable, interpretable baseline. The literature rarely reports confidence intervals or tests across classifiers; with small n, differences of a few percentage points are noise.

## f. Data leakage and specimen-level (group-wise) splitting

Kaufman et al. define leakage as the introduction of information about the target that should not be legitimately available, and discuss detection and avoidance [kaufman2012leakage]. Kapoor and Narayanan survey leakage across ML-based science and argue it is a driver of reproducibility failures [kapoor2023leakage]. Roberts et al. show that when data have hierarchical or grouped structure, random cross-validation gives over-optimistic error and blocked strategies are required [roberts2017cv]. A concrete empirical case from medical imaging: Tampu et al. found that splitting slices from the same subject across train and test inflated OCT classification metrics substantially, and recommend splitting at subject level [tampu2022leakage].

The structural analogue is direct. Images of the same specimen (several photos, video frames, load steps, augmentations) are correlated; if they land on both sides of a split, the classifier can recognise the specimen (its formwork pattern, its lighting, its crack history) rather than the failure mode. Public crack sets built from tiles share this structure: the METU/Ozgenel set derives 40,000 patches from 458 source images [ozgenel2019ccic], and the beam/column set of Del Savio et al. comes from frames extracted from lab videos [delsavio2025dataset]. Patch-level random splits on such data are therefore not independent.

I did not find an SHM paper that quantifies the leakage effect on crack images; the closest was a snippet describing whole-specimen assignment to test sets in a plaster-wall crack segmentation study, which I could not trace to a record (unverified.md). That absence is itself useful: the paper can state the gap and measure it, e.g., by reporting accuracy under a random image split versus a leave-specimen-out split on the same data. Leave-one-specimen-out or group k-fold, augmentation applied after splitting, and splitting by test series (specimens from one study share setup) are the recommended practice. Where specimens are few, report the per-fold spread, since small samples give large error bars.

## g. Public datasets

Crack/no-crack datasets are plentiful. SDNET2018 offers over 56,000 annotated patches of bridge decks, walls and pavements [dorafshan2018sdnet]. The METU/Ozgenel set has 40,000 227x227 images, balanced classes [ozgenel2019ccic; ozgenel2018performance]. CFD and Crack500 are road-pavement segmentation sets [shi2016cfd; yang2020feature]; DeepCrack provides segmentation data for varied surfaces [zou2019deepcrack]. PEER Hub ImageNet offers multi-attribute structural images, including damage-state labels [gao2020peer]. Bianchi and Hebdon catalogue visual inspection datasets [bianchi2022datasets].

Beam-specific data are scarce. Candidates are the Davoudi et al. data and code record (compiled from 17 published studies, CC BY 4.0) [davoudi2018data], the Del Savio et al. beam/column set with a single beam class [delsavio2025ulima], and a Mendeley record on beam-column joint and beam flexural tests with photographed crack propagation (not opened; see dataset-leads.md). None provides labelled flexural / shear / shear-flexural beam photographs. See dataset-leads.md for URLs, licences and download notes.

## h. Gap statement

The reviewed work leaves the following open.
1. Crack detection is mature, but pattern-level interpretation (what mechanism does this crack field indicate) for conventional RC beams is thinly covered. The closest work is load/damage estimation for shear-critical beams and slabs [davoudi2018structural; davoudi2019datadriven] and failure-mode identification for columns and joints [azhari2024crack; hamidia2022joints].
2. No public dataset provides labelled flexural / shear / shear-flexural beam crack images; the one beam-image failure dataset has a single beam class [delsavio2025dataset].
3. Evaluation practice: leakage is acknowledged in ML generally [kaufman2012leakage; kapoor2023leakage; roberts2017cv] and quantified in medical imaging [tampu2022leakage], but crack-image studies of structural members rarely state whether the split respects specimens, and I found none that quantifies the effect.
4. Reproducibility and interpretability: deep models dominate; a simple HOG-plus-classical-classifier baseline with a stated protocol (specimen-wise splits, fixed seeds, public code and data) is missing for conventional beams, and gives engineers features (orientation histograms) that map to the mechanics in section a.

A contribution fitted to this gap: a small, openly released, specimen-labelled set of beam crack-pattern images with a stated labelling rule; a HOG + SVM / RF / kNN / XGBoost baseline; and a side-by-side of random-image versus leave-specimen-out evaluation that shows how much reported accuracy depends on the split. Claims about beating deep models should be avoided unless tested.
